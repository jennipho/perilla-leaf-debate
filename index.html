The Setup

A meal?
Or a relationship test?

Picture this: You're at a Korean BBQ restaurant with a group of friends including your partner. Your partner is struggling to peel apart two perilla leaves that are stuck together — and an opposite-gender friend reaches over to help separate them.

And yet, when this question went viral on Korean social media and spread across TikTok, Instagram, and Reddit, it sparked an enormous debate. Some called it a simple act of courtesy. Others said it was a dealbreaker. Many couldn't even agree on what the disagreement was really about.

"Your partner's opposite-gender friend helps them peel apart two stuck perilla leaves with their chopsticks at KBBQ. Is that okay with you?"

— The question that launched a thousand arguments online

On the surface, it seems trivial. But I think the perilla leaf debate is actually a very interesting window into something much more fundamental in our relationship beliefs, such as - how our attachment styles and cultural conditioning shape what we experience as a threat to our relationships and what we don't.

The Research

What empirical evidence says about
jealousy & boundaries

The fuelled debate around this scenario is not random. Research in psychology gives us some reasoning to understand why some people find this completely innocuous and why others feel threatened or jealous!

🔗

Attachment Styles Shape Jealousy

People with anxious attachment experience more cognitive and emotional jealousy, while avoidantly attached individuals tend to withdraw and suppress. Securely attached people feel anger but can process it constructively.

Chursina, 2023
🌍

Culture Shapes How Jealousy Is Perceived

Across 11 societies, responses to perceived infidelity varied dramatically based on social norms — not just personality. In cultures where fathers are more involved in child-rearing, responses to relational threats tend to be significantly more intense.

Scelza et al., 2021 — UCLA
